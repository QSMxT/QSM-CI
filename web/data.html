The benchmark data

QSM is an ill-posed dipole deconvolution: the kernel vanishes on two cone surfaces in k-space, so the reconstruction is regulariser-dependent and different algorithms produce visibly different maps from the same phase. Comparing them needs a ground-truth susceptibility map, which no in vivo acquisition provides. This page covers why QSM-CI’s ground truth is synthetic, how the field arrived there, how to regenerate the dataset, and what the χ-separation phantom adds.

Assessing QSM algorithms

No in vivo acquisition yields a true susceptibility map, so QSM references are all surrogates, and each biases assessment differently.

In vitro phantoms (agar or aqueous solutions doped with known gadolinium or iron-oxide concentrations) give a known susceptibility, and established that QSM is linear and reproducible across sites; but their geometry and physics are far simpler than brain. In vivo, references are surrogates: literature deep-gray-matter iron over a few ROIs, or a COSMOS reconstruction (Liu et al., 2009) that over-determines the inversion by sampling multiple orientations, but averages anisotropic susceptibility across them, biasing it as a reference for single-orientation maps.

Dipole inversion has also been assessed against numerical or physical phantoms built from piecewise-constant susceptibility: geometric shapes or simplistic head models (de Rochefort et al., 2008; Wharton et al., 2010). This favours TV/TGV and morphological-prior methods, which reconstruct piecewise-constant maps almost by construction and so score well when the ground truth is itself piecewise-constant (Knoll et al., 2011), an edge that need not survive anisotropy, chemical exchange, physiological noise, flow, and microstructure. A ground truth is never neutral: its texture decides which assumptions get rewarded.

The QSM reconstruction challenges

Two community challenges, run at the International Workshop on MRI Phase Contrast & QSM, set out to assess dipole inversion systematically on common data.

The 2016 Reconstruction Challenge (Langkammer et al., 2018) used a single-orientation 3 T acquisition of one healthy volunteer and took the χ33 component of a susceptibility-tensor (STI) reconstruction as the reference, hoping to sidestep the orientation bias of COSMOS. Twenty-seven submissions were scored on RMSE, HFEN, SSIM and regional errors. The outcome was a warning about metrics as much as algorithms: because methods were tuned to minimise those errors, the winning maps were over-smoothed and lost fine structure such as vessels, and the top entries had near-identical scores but visibly different appearances. A follow-up analysis (Milovic et al., 2020) then showed the χ33 reference itself did not match the measured phase once anisotropy, microstructure, noise and residual background field were accounted for: the “ground truth” was not one.

Those lessons pushed the QSM Reconstruction Challenge 2.0 to drop in vivo references entirely in favour of an in silico head phantom (Marques et al., 2021): a realistic digital brain, derived from a 0.64 mm 7 T multi-parametric acquisition of a volunteer with an interhemispheric calcification, whose susceptibility map is known exactly and from which a data-consistent gradient-echo acquisition can be forward-simulated. Deliberately, the phantom builds in physiological texture and within-structure variation so it does not reward piecewise-smooth solutions the way earlier phantoms did. The challenge itself (QSM Challenge 2.0 committee, 2021) ran in two stages (stage 1 with no ground truth, mimicking the clinic; stage 2 with it released for parameter tuning) and scored NRMSE variants per region alongside calcification streaking and moment errors. Iterative methods with TV-based regularisation scored best, and methods that used multi-echo and magnitude information beat those that did not.

What QSM-CI does

Both challenges were fixed events: a submission window, a closed field of entries, and each reconstruction judged as a finished whole. What outlasts them is the more useful artefact, a public phantom with a genuine, per-voxel ground truth. QSM-CI turns that ground truth into a standing benchmark, forward-simulated to a BIDS acquisition with qsm-forward. Because every intermediate is exactly known (the total field, the local field, the tissue susceptibility, and for source separation the paramagnetic and diamagnetic components), it scores each pipeline stage on its own, field-mapping, background removal and dipole inversion, rather than only the end result, and it stays open: methods are submitted, rerun and ranked as they appear.

It stays synthetic, but less idealised than the challenge as it was run. Because qsm-forward simulates the whole-head susceptibility, the data carries realistic background fields (from air-tissue interfaces) and wrapped multi-echo phase, so the field-mapping and background-removal stages genuinely have to unwrap phase and remove background fields, not just invert the dipole kernel the challenges scored. What the model still leaves out is the harder physics: susceptibility is an isotropic scalar (no white-matter anisotropy), there is no flow or motion, microstructure enters only through the χ-separation compartments, noise is complex Gaussian, and the phantom derives from a single subject. A QSM-CI ranking is therefore a strong controlled test, not a guarantee of in vivo performance.

One inheritance from RC2 is visible in the results. The phantom deliberately gives brain tissue realistic texture and within-structure variation to discourage piecewise-smooth reconstructions, yet TV-based methods still lead the dipole-inversion leaderboard, as they topped every metric in RC2. The rest of this page is how to regenerate the data and what the χ-separation variant adds.

1. The source phantom

qsm-forward does not ship the anatomy. It simulates an acquisition from a set of ground-truth tissue-property maps: the digital brain model published with the QSM Reconstruction Challenge 2.0. You request those maps from the authors (Donders / Radboud University data repository) and point qsm-forward at them.

Request the maps

QSM Challenge 2.0 dataset (Marques et al.) in the Donders Repository: data.ru.nl/collections/di/dccn/DSC_3015069.02_542 (DOI 10.34973/m20r-jt17). Accept the data-use terms to download the archive.

Arrange the download so qsm-forward’s head mode finds the maps it expects: 

qsm-challenge-2.0/
├── chimodel/
│   ├── ChiModelMIX.nii            # ground-truth susceptibility (χ_total)
│   └── SusceptibilityValues.mat   # per-label χ⁺/χ⁻ source values (Tables 1–2)
├── maps/
│   ├── M0.nii.gz  R1.nii.gz  R2star.nii.gz
└── masks/
    └── BrainMask.nii.gz

2. Install qsm-forward

pip install qsm-forward     # a virtualenv or conda env is recommended
qsm-forward --help

qsm-forward is open source (astewartau/qsm-forward). The CLI wraps the same generate_bids() Python API the sections below describe.

3. Generate the QSM phantom

This is the dataset behind the main leaderboard (the field-mapping, background-field removal, dipole inversion, and end-to-end tracks). It simulates a 7 T multi-echo GRE acquisition and writes the total and local field maps as ground truth. 

# 7 T, 4 echoes (4/12/20/28 ms), 1 mm, fixed seed: matches the scored phantom
qsm-forward head ./qsm-challenge-2.0 bids_qsm --save-field

--save-field emits the total field (fieldmap) and local field (fieldmap-local) that the isolated BFR and dipole stages are scored against. Acquisition parameters (--B0, --TEs, --voxel-size, --peak-snr, --random-seed) all default to the challenge phantom; override them for sensitivity studies.

4. Generate the χ-separation phantom

Susceptibility source-separation methods split the net susceptibility χ into a paramagnetic component χ+ (iron) and a diamagnetic component χ− (myelin, calcium). The χ-sep phantom adds those source maps, an R2′ map, and a source-separation-aware signal model. 

qsm-forward head ./qsm-challenge-2.0 bids_chisep \
  --save-field --save-chi-pos --save-chi-neg --save-r2prime \
  --chisep-signal --chisep-multicompartment
  • --save-chi-pos / --save-chi-neg: the χ+ and χ− ground-truth source maps (the scoring targets).
  • --save-r2prime: the R2′ map, a provided input for R2′-domain methods (χ-sepnet, SUSEP-Net, χ-sep iLSQR/MEDI, WaveSep).
  • --chisep-signal: replaces the plain R2* magnitude decay with the χ-sep static-dephasing model (see §6).
  • --chisep-multicompartment: builds the voxel magnitude as a sum of paramagnetic, diamagnetic, and neutral compartments, which signal-domain separators (e.g. DECOMPOSE) need (see §6). It implies --chisep-signal.

5. Run methods on it

qsm-forward writes a standard BIDS tree, so there is nothing to repackage. Point qsm-ci run straight at the individual NIfTIs. It reads acquisition parameters from the BIDS JSON sidecar and stacks per-echo files for you. The ground-truth maps sit alongside in derivatives/qsm-forward/, so you can score against them with --truth too. 

# field-based χ-sep method: inputs are individual files from the BIDS tree
D=bids_chisep/derivatives/qsm-forward/sub-1/anat
qsm-ci run chi-sepnet \
  --localfield $D/sub-1_fieldmap-local.nii --r2prime $D/sub-1_R2prime.nii \
  --chimap $D/sub-1_Chimap.nii --mask $D/sub-1_mask.nii \
  --params bids_chisep/sub-1/anat/sub-1_echo-1_part-phase_MEGRE.json

Each method’s inputs depend on its stage. Run it with no inputs and it prints exactly what to provide. See Run for the full CLI, scoring, and chaining stages into a pipeline.

6. What the χ-separation phantom does differently

A χ-separation phantom is the QSM phantom plus a forward model for how paramagnetic and diamagnetic sources dephase the MR signal. The Challenge 2.0 phantom ships χtotal, R2*, R1, M0 and a tissue segmentation, but no source-separated maps and no R2′; the χ-sep phantom derives them. χtotal is split into paramagnetic (χ⁺) and diamagnetic (χ⁻) components from per-tissue reference values and the segmentation, and R2′ follows from that split, so everything the benchmark scores against traces back to the challenge susceptibility map, not its R2*. The forward model then builds up in two layers: first the field-domain machinery that most methods need (how R2′ relates to R2, and the relaxivity Dr that sets it), then the multi-compartment magnitude that signal-domain separators additionally rely on.

Relaxation: R2, R2*, and R2′

The gradient-echo magnitude decays at R2* = R2 + R2′. R2 is the irreversible rate (spin–spin interactions, diffusion); R2′ is the reversible rate from static field inhomogeneity around susceptibility sources, which a spin echo refocuses. R2′ is the susceptibility-driven part of the decay, and hence the relaxation channel source separation draws on.

The phantom simulates R2 independently, from per-tissue literature T2 values (with the challenge R2* map only lending realistic intra-tissue texture), and adds it to R2′ to reach R2*, with no fixed R2*/R2′ ratio assumed. The R2′ map is then shipped directly as a provided input (r2prime.nii.gz). qsm-forward can instead simulate a matched multi-echo spin echo (--save-se), whose magnitude decays with R2 alone (the 180° pulse refocuses static dephasing), letting a method recover R2′ = R2* − R2 itself as it would from real data (Stoll, 2025); the ground-truth R2 and R2′ are the same either way, so this phantom simply ships R2′ directly and does not currently include the SE.

Why R2 and R2′ are modelled independently

“Independently” means R2 (from tissue T2 values) and R2′ (Dr·(|χ⁺| + |χ⁻|), from the source magnitudes) are generated from separate maps, so neither is a function of the other. A common shortcut in χ-sep forward models is the κ approximation, R2* ≈ κ·R2′ (κ ≈ 1.9; Dimov et al., 2022; adopted by Stoll 2025): with no independent R2, it takes R2 as a fixed multiple of R2′ (R2 = (κ−1)·R2′ ≈ 0.9·R2′). We avoid it: tying R2 to R2′ by a constant makes the two collinear, so recovering R2′ = R2* − R2 becomes degenerate and unrealistically easy, and R2 carries no tissue information of its own. κ ≈ 1.9 is kept only as a sanity check on the phantom’s mean R2*/R2′ ratio.

The relaxivity Dr

Relaxivity is the constant that turns a susceptibility source into the reversible relaxation it produces: how many Hz of R2′ you get per ppm of source. χ-separation models it with a single kernel shared by both source types: 

R2′ = D_r · ( |χ⁺| + |χ⁻| ),   D_r = 137 Hz/ppm

One kernel for both, because in the static-dephasing regime (Yablonskiy & Haacke, 1994) reversible relaxation depends on the magnitude of the field perturbation, not its sign, so a paramagnetic and a diamagnetic source of equal strength dephase spins equally. Dr = 137 Hz/ppm is the value Shin et al. (2021) measured, and it is the standard χ-separation setting.

Why not a split (Dr+ ≠ Dr−)?

Real microstructure (spherical ferritin vs. anisotropic myelin) plausibly gives the two source types different effective relaxivities, so a split is biophysically defensible in principle. It is deliberately not used here because:

  • The relaxivities are not recoverable from the data. With one QSM and one R2′ map they must be assumed a priori, so baking a specific split into the ground truth mainly rewards whichever method shares that exact assumption rather than testing recovery.
  • The methods benchmarked here invert a single-kernel model, so a shared kernel keeps the ground truth aligned with the model they assume rather than pre-committing it to one particular split.
  • A split remains available as an explicit opt-in (generate_r2prime(..., dr_neg=…) or --dr-neg) for sensitivity studies.

The multi-compartment magnitude

Field-domain methods only need R2′ and the field. Signal-domain separators (e.g. DECOMPOSE) instead fit the multi-echo complex signal per voxel, and a plain mono-exponential decay carries no information to separate. With --chisep-multicompartment the voxel magnitude becomes the modulus of a sum of compartments: 

S(TE) = | C₊·e^(−(R2 + D_r|χ⁺| + i·ω·χ⁺)·TE)
        + C₋·e^(−(R2 + D_r|χ⁻| + i·ω·χ⁻)·TE)
        +  C₀·e^(−R2·TE) |,     ω = (2/3)·γ·B₀

Each compartment is a static-dephasing exponential with its own decay rate and off-resonance, so the paramagnetic and diamagnetic pools beat against each other and give the non-mono-exponential magnitude those methods rely on. Crucially, the compartment decay rates reuse the same Dr kernel as R2′, so the effective R2* (and thus R2′) is unchanged: the field-domain methods see identical inputs, and only the shape of the magnitude decay is enriched. It defaults to off.
